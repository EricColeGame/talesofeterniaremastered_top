## A.L.M.B.S. Basics: How 2D Battles Flow in Real Time

The "linear" in Linear Motion Battle System is literal. You move along a line relative to your current target, which means you must close the distance before a melee attack will connect. Attack too early from across the screen and your character swings at air while enemies close in.

The practical loop is simple: approach, land a short normal-attack chain, then cancel the final hit into an arte. Guard when you are out of position, and let your AI-controlled allies cover the space you cannot. In the original, you could also set the character you control to Manual, Semi-Auto, or Auto — Semi-Auto automatically walks you toward the target, which is a useful training wheel for newcomers.

| Action | How it behaves | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Normal attack chain | A short sequence of melee hits, typically three | Sets up the cancel into an arte |
| Base artes | Low TP cost, fast startup | Bread-and-butter damage and combo filler |
| Arcane artes and spells | Higher TP cost, longer animations | Big damage, but you are exposed if you mistime the cast |
| Guard | Hold to reduce incoming damage | Resets a losing exchange and buys time for allies |
| Control mode | Manual, Semi-Auto, or Auto | Manual gives full movement control; Semi-Auto closes distance for you |
| Target switching | Cycle between enemies with a dedicated input | Keeps your combo on the target that actually matters |

One habit worth building immediately: watch the end of your attack chain, not the enemy's health bar. The moment your third hit lands is your window to cancel.

## Artes, TP, and Combo Routing That Actually Works

TP is the real currency of any fight. Every arte and spell draws from the same pool, and in the original game that pool refills slowly. Blow it on trash mobs and you will enter the boss room with nothing left for healing.

Combo routing is how you stretch that pool. A short normal chain into a base arte costs little and clears most random encounters. Save arcane artes and big spells for staggered enemies, cornered targets, or boss windows where the damage actually lands.

Magic works differently from melee artes. In the original, spellcasters learned their spell lists by assigning Craymels to Craymel Cages — a system that tied character progression to exploration and party composition. Expect the remaster to preserve that structure unless Bandai Namco announces otherwise.

| Arte type | Typical TP cost | Best moment to use | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base arte | Low | Right after two or three normal attacks | Minimal — safe combo filler |
| Arcane arte | Medium to high | When the enemy is staggered or cornered | Whiffing leaves you wide open |
| Offensive spell | Medium to high | While allies hold the front line | Long cast time, easily interrupted |
| Healing spell | Medium to high | Before a damage spike, not after | Casting too late wastes the TP |
| Buff or support arte | Low to medium | At the start of a boss phase | Wasted if the phase ends quickly |

| Situation | Suggested route | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Common enemies | Three normals → base arte | Fastest clear with minimal TP spent |
| Tanky mid-boss | Two normals → base arte → arcane arte | Maximizes damage per opening |
| Surrounded | Guard → reposition → single base arte | Avoids getting pinned against the line |
| Boss phase change | Buff → heal → resume combos | Prepares the party for the next window |
| Low on TP | Normals only, then a gel | Preserves TP for the burst window |

Community reports from the original game consistently highlight one lesson: players who treat TP as a resource to manage rather than a bar to empty finish boss fights far more comfortably.

## Party Roles, AI, and Co-op Expectations

You control one character. Everyone else is run by the game's AI, guided by a strategy or formation menu that lets you decide how aggressively allies attack, when they heal, and how freely they spend TP. Setting that menu well is often the difference between a clean run and a wipe.

Role assignment matters more than raw stats. A caster parked next to a boss will get interrupted constantly; the same caster positioned behind a frontline fighter will land every spell.

| Role | Typical member | Battle job | AI setting to consider |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frontline swordsman | Reid | Holds the line and opens combo windows | Aggressive; attack freely |
| Fast melee | Farah | Short-range pressure and juggles | Aggressive, but stay near the leader |
| Offensive caster | Keele or Meredy | Spell damage from a safe distance | Stay back; conserve TP |
| Healer | Whoever carries the healing Craymels | Keeps the party standing | Prioritize healing; low TP use |
| Ranged or support | Later recruits | Chip damage and utility | Balanced |

On multiplayer: the official listing tags the game as single-player. The 2001 release supported local co-op with additional controllers, but that is not advertised here. If group play is a dealbreaker for you, wait for confirmation rather than assuming.

## Tips, Drills, and What Veterans Should Relearn

Veterans have the steepest adjustment curve, because muscle memory from 2001 does not always translate to a smoother presentation. The fundamentals hold, but the habits around them often need pruning.

| Common habit | Better approach | Payoff |
|---|---|---|
| Mashing attack until the combo ends | Cancel the third hit into a base arte | More damage, fewer whiffs |
| Casting the instant a spell is ready | Reposition behind your frontline first | Fewer interrupted casts |
| Hoarding gels and life bottles | Spend them early while learning a fight | Fewer full-party wipes |
| Letting the AI burn TP freely | Set allies to conserve TP on long fights | Healing available when it counts |
| Using one route for every encounter | Match the combo route to the enemy type | Faster clears, less attrition |

A simple five-step drill plan for your first ten hours:

1. Practice guard timing against the weakest enemies in the starting area until it feels automatic.
2. Repeat "two normals into a base arte" until you stop thinking about the inputs.
3. Run the same fight three times, once in each control mode, to find your comfort setting.
4. Fight one boss with casters set to "stay back" and compare it to your default setup.
5. Check your TP after every battle — if you consistently finish below roughly a third, tighten your routing.

## FAQ

**What combat system does Tales of Eternia Remastered use?**
The official site names it the Aggressive Linear Motion Battle System, or A.L.M.B.S. — a real-time 2D system where you control one character while AI allies fight alongside you.

**Is the Tales of Eternia Remastered combat system turn-based?**
No. There is no turn queue. You move, attack, guard, and cast continuously, and enemies act on their own timing.

**Does the remaster add new artes or change damage values?**
That has not been detailed publicly. The official page mentions updated visuals plus enhanced playability and quality-of-life improvements, so treat balance changes as unconfirmed until launch-day reports appear.

**Can you play it with friends?**
The official listing describes it as single-player. The original 2001 release allowed local multiplayer battles, but that feature is not advertised for the remaster, so confirm before buying for co-op.
